Daily edition · November 19, 2026

Have you seen AI in Criminal Justice Today?

Michigan will start scoring prison security levels with new software on Dec. 1. In three US cases, courts and police are fighting over whether AI output can justify an arrest.

Five verified developments. Michigan's corrections department plans to pilot a redesigned, software-driven classification system built with the nonprofit Recidiviz; both say the scores are rules-based, not AI. In Arizona and Kentucky, judges refused to treat AI output alone as probable cause, and Fargo is defending a $10 million false-arrest suit. From the archive: a Tasmanian parole condition struck down after the board relied on an AI-assisted document citing case law that did not exist, and the UK justice ministry's one-year AI report listing tools now live in probation and prisons. Added in a later update: in Indiana, a $200,000 OpenAI Foundation grant will add an AI literacy course to a job program inside three women's prisons.

Lead story

Corrections & PrisonsNorth AmericaPilot

Michigan will pilot a new scoring system for prison security levels on Dec. 1, and its developer says AI plays no part in the score

The Michigan Department of Corrections says the redesigned classification system, built with the nonprofit Recidiviz, should predict future violence about 30% better. It has not said how it reached that figure.

Two US judges refuse to let AI output stand in for probable cause, as Fargo defends a $10 million false-arrest suit

An Arizona judge released a DUI suspect because an AI tool helped write the arrest report. A Kentucky federal judge let a wrongful-arrest suit over a face match proceed. Fargo says its officers broke no law.

An OpenAI Foundation grant will add an AI literacy course to a job program inside three Indiana women's prisons

The Televerde Foundation received $200,000 to build the course and an AI-powered resource center for graduates of its PATHS reentry program, Free Press Indiana reports.
