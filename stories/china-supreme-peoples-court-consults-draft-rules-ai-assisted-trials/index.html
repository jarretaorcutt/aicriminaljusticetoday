China's Supreme People's Court consults on draft rules for AI-assisted trials after piloting its own large model in some courts

Court leaders say judges must stay in charge and efficiency must never be pursued one-sidedly, according to the court's news service.

China's Supreme People's Court held two consultation meetings on a draft document titled Opinions on Regulating AI-Assisted Adjudication Work (关于规范人工智能辅助审判工作的意见), according to a report from the courts' news service posted on the court's website on 2026-11-21. Court President Zhang Jun (张军) presided.

The report says the court has developed an AI-assisted trial application using a large model with its own intellectual property, trained on laws, judicial interpretations, normative documents and large numbers of cases, and has piloted it in some intermediate and basic-level courts through its unified case-handling system. The court describes the pilot as having achieved interim results; that is the court's own assessment.

Participants included national legislators, political advisers, scholars, lawyers and officials from central bodies, among them the Supreme People's Procuratorate, the Ministry of Public Security and the Ministry of Justice. According to the report, they agreed on principles including that judges lead and technology assists (法官主导、技术辅助), that efficiency must never be pursued one-sidedly (决不能片面追求效率), and that the judicial accountability system must be strictly enforced. Suggestions recorded in the report included ethics testing before deployment, monitoring during operation and a separate chapter on red lines.

The draft is still being written. The report gives no release date, no list of pilot courts and no performance figures.

What we know

  • The Supreme People's Court held two consultation meetings on a draft opinion regulating AI-assisted adjudication (court report, 2026-11-21).
  • The court says it piloted an AI trial-assistance application built on its own large model in some intermediate and basic-level courts (court report).
  • Stated principles include judge-led, technology-assisted adjudication and strict judicial accountability (court report).

What remains unknown

  • Which courts ran the pilot, for what tasks, and with what measured results.
  • When the opinion will be issued and what its red lines will be.

Benefits and opportunities

  • A written rule could set limits before wider rollout rather than after.

Risks and limitations

  • Results reported only by the court itself are hard to verify independently.
