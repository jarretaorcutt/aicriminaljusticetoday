The Met Police starts a six-month pilot of a handheld facial recognition tool used by 100 trained officers

Operator Initiated Facial Recognition compares a photo taken on a Met-issued mobile device with the force's custody image database, in custody suites and on the frontline.

Jurisdiction

The Metropolitan Police Service has launched a six-month pilot of Operator Initiated Facial Recognition (OIFR), a handheld tool being trialled by 100 trained officers across custody suites and frontline policing, the force announced on 20 November 2026.

According to the Met, the system compares a photo taken by an officer on a Met-issued mobile device against the Met's custody image database. It returns a result only where it finds a match above a threshold the Met says was informed by independent testing; otherwise no result is returned.

The Met says OIFR can be used only in two specific circumstances: to help identify someone unknown to the officer who is believed to have committed a recordable offence and who refuses to give details or is believed to have given false ones, and after an arrest or detention to help confirm identity and support risk assessments. Commander Nick John, the Met's lead for OIFR, said every potential match is reviewed by a trained officer and that officers must first exhaust other reasonable ways of establishing a person's identity.

The Met says the pilot will be monitored with regular reviews of performance, accuracy and usage data, including for any evidence of disproportionality. A UK Information Commissioner's Office audit of the Met's facial recognition, published the same week, did not assess this tool because it was not yet operational.

What we know

  • The pilot runs for six months and involves 100 trained officers (Metropolitan Police).
  • Photos taken on a Met-issued mobile device are compared with the Met's custody image database (Metropolitan Police).
  • The Met says use is limited to two specific circumstances and every potential match is reviewed by a trained officer (Metropolitan Police).

What remains unknown

  • The matching threshold the Met uses and the independent testing behind it.
  • Whether the Met will publish the pilot's accuracy and disproportionality results.

Benefits and opportunities

  • The Met says faster identification can flag risks such as a history of carrying a weapon and reduce unnecessary time in custody (Metropolitan Police).

Risks and limitations

  • Handheld face matching moves facial recognition from fixed deployments into individual street encounters, where errors could affect a stop or arrest.
