Colorado gives parole officers an optional AI tool that drafts their meeting notes; the state says it makes no supervision decisions

The corrections department says officers had used the Recidiviz tool to document more than 800 meetings by Nov. 4 and must approve every draft note.

The Colorado Department of Corrections says its community parole officers can now use an AI tool before, during and after meetings with the people they supervise. In a release dated November 4, 2026, the department said it has launched the Meetings module built by Recidiviz, a technology nonprofit that the release says is active in 19 states.

According to the department, the tool gives an officer client-specific information before a meeting, captures details during it and drafts the case note, and afterward gives the officer feedback. The release says officers must review, edit, and approve all draft notes before they become part of the official record.

The department draws a line around what the tool does: it "does not make parole decisions, determine supervision levels, or recommend enforcement actions." It says using the tool is optional, that it has held more than a dozen trainings, and that as of November 4, 2026 officers had used it to document more than 800 meetings. Outputs remain the department's property and are not used to train AI models, the release says.

Executive Director André Stancil said the tool reduces administrative demands on officers. The release also quotes a parole team leader, Daniel Chavez, who said it lets him focus on the person in front of him.

This account comes from the agency that adopted the tool. The release does not include any view from people on parole, and it gives no accuracy figures for the drafted notes.

What we know

  • Colorado's corrections department launched Recidiviz's Meetings module for community parole officers (Colorado Department of Corrections release, Nov. 4, 2026).
  • Officers had documented more than 800 meetings with it as of Nov. 4, and use is optional (Colorado Department of Corrections).
  • The department says the tool makes no parole, supervision-level or enforcement recommendations and that officers must approve each draft note (Colorado Department of Corrections).

What remains unknown

  • Whether people on parole are told their meetings are being captured, and whether they can decline.
  • How accurate the drafted notes are; the release gives no error rates.
  • What the pilot found before the tool was offered to all parole officers.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Less time on paperwork could mean more attention in the meeting itself, as the department argues.
  • Human approval of every note keeps an officer responsible for the record.

Risks and limitations

  • Case notes can be used in revocation decisions, so an error in a draft that an officer misses could matter.
  • The only public account so far is the agency's own.
