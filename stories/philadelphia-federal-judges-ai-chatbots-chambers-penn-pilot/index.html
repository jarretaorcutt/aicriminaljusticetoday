Around 25 judges are testing AI chatbots in their chambers through a Penn law school pilot, the Inquirer reports

Judges in the federal court in Philadelphia use the tools to schedule hearings and draft jury instructions. The professor who runs the program says the tools are "pretty bad at legal research."

A group of federal judges in Philadelphia are using artificial intelligence to help with cases in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania, The Philadelphia Inquirer reported on Nov. 20, 2026.

The judges are part of a pilot run by researchers at the University of Pennsylvania's Carey Law School called the PennLaw-Judiciary AI Testbed. According to the Inquirer, participants have used AI chatbots to schedule court hearings, draft jury instructions and, in some cases, recall and summarize factual information about their cases. In all, around 25 judges in Pennsylvania and surrounding states are taking part. The program has grown from fewer than 10 chambers when it began in 2024.

Polk Wagner, the Penn law professor who oversees the program, told the Inquirer that participants largely use the tools to speed up administrative tasks and that his team encourages chambers to keep their use as limited as possible. "These tools are pretty bad at legal research - even today," he said.

U.S. District Judge Joshua D. Wolson told the Inquirer that he uses AI to help wordsmith jury instructions while his law clerks handle the legal analysis, and that he refrains from using it for legal research, summarizing documents or drafting opinions. Zachary Arbitman, cochair of the Philadelphia Bar Association's federal courts committee, told the paper he is not aware of any court-wide rules on judicial AI use.

What we know

  • Around 25 judges in Pennsylvania and surrounding states are taking part in the PennLaw-Judiciary AI Testbed (The Philadelphia Inquirer, Nov. 20, 2026).
  • Uses include scheduling hearings, drafting jury instructions and, in some cases, summarizing factual information about cases (Inquirer).
  • The program began in 2024 with fewer than 10 chambers (Inquirer).
  • Judge Joshua D. Wolson says he does not use AI for legal research, summarizing documents or drafting opinions (Inquirer).

What remains unknown

  • Which judges besides Judge Wolson take part, and which tasks each chambers uses AI for.
  • Whether parties are told when AI was used on work in their case.
  • Whether the court will adopt court-wide rules on judicial AI use.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Judges and staff get training and support before using the tools on live work.
  • Faster routine drafting, such as jury instructions, according to Judge Wolson.

Risks and limitations

  • Generative AI can produce answers that sound correct but are inaccurate, the Inquirer notes.
  • No court-wide rule on judicial AI use was identified in the report.
