Ethnic minority people made up 24% of South Wales Police's officer-initiated face scans in 2025, against 7% of the population

The force says the figures raise concerns about disproportionality and it is reviewing its approach, the Guardian reports from data obtained by Big Brother Watch.

South Wales Police officers capture a person's face with a smartphone and run it through an algorithm called Neoface to match against custody mugshots and other databases, a practice the force calls officer-initiated facial recognition (OIFR). Figures for 2025, obtained under the Freedom of Information Act by Big Brother Watch and shared with the Guardian, show 24% of people selected for these scans were from ethnic minorities, the Guardian reported on Nov. 15, 2026. Ethnic minority people make up 7% of the area's population and 11% of people arrested there.

According to the data, officers initiated face scans of 257 adults and 14 children in 2025. More than half were suspected of a crime, being unlawfully at large or breaching bail conditions. Where a match was made, arrests followed in 48% of cases and no further action resulted in a third of cases.

"We recognise the figures raise concerns about disproportionality," a South Wales Police spokesperson told the Guardian, adding that the use of the technology is in its infancy; the force said it was reviewing its approach. Jake Hurfurt of Big Brother Watch called the figures alarming, and Aliya Mohammed of Race Equality First said they raised serious concerns about racial disproportionality.

The Guardian also spoke with a 26-year-old man who was arrested in February for a burglary in a city he had never visited after face-scanning software confused him with someone else on a custody database. A similar handheld system is being trialled by the Metropolitan police in London.

What we know

  • 24% of people selected for South Wales Police OIFR scans in 2025 were from ethnic minorities, versus 7% of the population and 11% of arrestees (Guardian, from FOI data).
  • Officers initiated scans of 257 adults and 14 children in 2025 (Guardian).
  • Where a match was made, arrests followed in 48% of cases (Guardian).
  • The force says it recognises concerns about disproportionality and is reviewing its approach (Guardian).

What remains unknown

  • What changes, if any, the force's review will make.
  • How often the system returned a wrong match; the figures reported cover who was scanned, not error rates.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Officers can identify people who refuse to give details or who are unconscious, missing or dead, according to the force's stated purposes.

Risks and limitations

  • Who gets scanned depends on officer choice, so existing bias in stops can carry into the face database searches.
  • A wrong match against custody images can lead to arrest of the wrong person.
