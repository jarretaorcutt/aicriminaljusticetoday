New York Senate bill S.10741 would make a judge's knowing or reckless reliance on fabricated legal authority professional misconduct

The bill's sponsor, Sen. Michelle Hinchey, says AI use by a judge would not by itself be misconduct.

By the AI in Criminal Justice Today newsroom · Published November 27, 2026
Story type
News
Jurisdiction
United States, New York
AI application
Generative AI legal research and drafting by judges

The Post-Journal reported on November 27, 2026 that Sen. Michelle Hinchey, D-Kingston, introduced legislation (S.10741) that would add a new section to the state Judiciary Law prohibiting judges from using fabricated legal authority in adjudicative writings. A judge who knowingly or recklessly includes, relies on or adopts such authority would be engaging in misconduct. The use of AI by a judge would not, by itself, constitute misconduct, and errors based solely on good-faith interpretation or immaterial typographic or clerical errors would not count. The state Commission on Judicial Conduct would have to weigh factors including the number and materiality of fabricated authorities and whether errors affected the disposition of the case. The article notes that New York's Unified Court System recently released an Interim Policy on the Use of Artificial Intelligence, and that Senate Judiciary Committee Chairman Chuck Grassley raised federal judges' AI use in October 2025. It also cites a Northwestern University survey of federal judges in which more than 60% reported using at least one AI tool in their judicial work and 45.5% said AI training was not provided by court administration. Hinchey wrote in her legislative justification that such failures can affect rights including detention and liberty.

What we know

  • S.10741 would add a section to New York's Judiciary Law on fabricated legal authority in judges' adjudicative writings (Post-Journal, Nov. 27, 2026).
  • AI use by a judge would not by itself be misconduct under the bill (Post-Journal).
  • A Northwestern University survey found more than 60% of responding federal judges used at least one AI tool, and 45.5% said AI training was not provided (Post-Journal).

What remains unknown

  • Whether the bill will receive a committee hearing or vote.
  • We have not read the bill text; it is not yet reviewed by us.

Benefits and opportunities

  • The bill sets a misconduct standard before a New York court harm occurs, while keeping lawful AI use permitted.

Risks and limitations

  • The article does not say how the Commission on Judicial Conduct would detect fabricated citations.
