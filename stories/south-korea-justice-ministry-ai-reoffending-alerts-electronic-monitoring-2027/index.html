South Korea's justice ministry plans to pilot an AI system in 2027 that warns of reoffending risk among people on electronic monitoring

The system would analyze location data, crime information and interview records of sex offenders under electronic monitoring; officers already carry about 20 cases each, the ministry says.

South Korea's Ministry of Justice will begin test-running an 'AI recidivism prediction system' at major offices from 2027, the Seoul Shinmun reported on Nov. 3, 2026. According to material the ministry submitted to the office of the chair of the National Assembly's Legislation and Judiciary Committee, the system would analyze the location, crime information and interview records of sex offenders under electronic monitoring and alert staff to reoffending risk. The ministry has been developing AI-based electronic monitoring technology since 2024, the paper reported.

The ministry also plans to reorganize monitoring staff into teams, overhaul its classification system with regular risk assessments, and seek law changes that would make a ban on carrying weapons a required condition for people convicted of serious violent crimes such as murder, and create a 'temporary measure' letting probation officers step in quickly. The Seoul Shinmun linked those measures to a knife attack in Incheon last month by a man wearing an electronic ankle tag, which injured 8 people.

Staffing remains the constraint, the ministry told lawmakers. Total electronic monitoring staff grew from 450 in 2022 to 521 in September, but the increase went to officers handling high-risk sex offenders one-to-one; officers supervising everyone else fell from 255 to 234, leaving each with about 20 people. The ministry said it would consult other agencies to reach an appropriate caseload of 10 per officer.

What we know

  • The ministry plans to test-run an AI system that warns of reoffending risk at major offices from 2027 (Seoul Shinmun, Nov. 3, 2026).
  • It would analyze location, crime information and interview records of sex offenders under electronic monitoring (Seoul Shinmun).
  • Electronic monitoring staff rose from 450 in 2022 to 521 in September, while general supervision officers fell from 255 to 234, about 20 people each (Seoul Shinmun, citing the ministry).
  • The material was submitted by the ministry to the office of the National Assembly's Legislation and Judiciary Committee chair; we have not seen it directly (Seoul Shinmun).

What remains unknown

  • How accurate the system is, what data it was trained on, and what officers must do when it raises an alert.
  • Whether the proposed law changes will pass the National Assembly.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Alerts could help stretched officers focus on the cases that most need attention.

Risks and limitations

  • Continuous AI analysis of location and interview records could flag people for behavior that is not a violation, and the article describes no accuracy testing or appeal process.
