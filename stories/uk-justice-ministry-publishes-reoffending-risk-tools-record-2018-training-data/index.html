UK justice ministry publishes how its reoffending-risk tools work: scores used in pre-sentence and parole reports come from models trained on a 2018 caseload

A new government transparency record describes five tools that prison and probation staff calculate daily, and says the models have no fixed retraining schedule.

The UK government's Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard register gained a record on 19 November 2026 for the HMPPS Risk of Reoffending Tools, owned by the Ministry of Justice and His Majesty's Prison and Probation Service. It is a primary document, written by the department that runs the tools, and it sets out in unusual detail how risk scores used across sentencing, prison and probation in England and Wales are produced.

The record says the tools are made up of five algorithmic sub-tools. Each estimates the likelihood of proven reoffending within 2 years: for any recordable offence, for violent offences, for serious violent offences, for specified sexual offences, and a combined serious score. The record lists eight model specifications underneath them. Most are logistic regression models, which the ministry says it chose because the approach is among the simplest and most interpretable.

According to the record, the scores are an input to pre-sentence reports considered at sentencing in court, to Risk of Serious Harm assessments, to supervision levels for people managed in the community and to allocation to intervention programmes. It says scores can be presented in formal written reports such as pre-sentence reports, parole reports and recall decisions, and that thousands of prison and probation staff calculate them daily. Police use the sexual reoffending sub-tool for people with notification requirements.

On training data, the record says the models were trained on a snapshot dataset of the HMPPS community caseload as of 30 June 2018 (189,097 people in the raw static training set) and tested on people who started the community part of a sentence between 01 July to 31 December 2018. The models for all, violent and serious violent reoffending carry a February 2026 release version; the two sexual reoffending models carry an April 2024 release. One of them, the dynamic all-reoffending model, is described as deployed operationally for the first time. The record states: "The models do not have a fixed retraining schedule." Several replace predecessors released in 2009.

The record reports accuracy as a c-index, where 0.5 is random guessing and 1 is perfect ordering. Figures in its performance table run from 0.756 to 0.812.

On fairness, the record says the protected characteristics used as model features are age and gender, and that women receive no sexual reoffending score because too few cases existed to build a model. It says an equality analysis was completed and rated the identified impacts as "neither or limited" across the protected characteristics considered. The record gives no performance figures by ethnicity or other group, and it names two data protection impact assessments (DPIA ID 12980 and DPIA ID 8359) without linking them. No third party was involved in building the tools, it says.

The ministry stresses that the scores support rather than automate decisions: "the decision ultimately sits with the judicial office holder / probation officer."

What we know

  • The record was published on 19 November 2026 and covers five algorithmic sub-tools built on eight model specifications (GOV.UK transparency record).
  • Scores feed pre-sentence reports, parole reports, recall decisions, supervision levels and programme allocation, and thousands of prison and probation staff calculate them daily (GOV.UK transparency record).
  • The models were trained on a snapshot of the community caseload as of 30 June 2018, and the record says they have no fixed retraining schedule (GOV.UK transparency record).
  • Age and gender are model features; an equality analysis rated impacts "neither or limited" (GOV.UK transparency record).
  • Reported c-index figures run from 0.756 to 0.812 (GOV.UK transparency record).

What remains unknown

  • How the models perform for different ethnic groups; the record gives overall figures only and does not link the equality analysis.
  • When, or against what newer data, the models will next be re-tested.
  • How much weight judges, parole panels and probation officers give the scores in practice.

Benefits and opportunities

  • A public, technical description lets defence lawyers, researchers and courts check what a risk score is built from.
  • Simple, published model types are easier to explain and challenge than opaque systems.

Risks and limitations

  • Training data from 2018 may not reflect who is under supervision now, and no retraining schedule is set.
  • Without published results by group, outsiders cannot check the equality finding.
  • The record itself lists over-reliance on scores as a risk.
