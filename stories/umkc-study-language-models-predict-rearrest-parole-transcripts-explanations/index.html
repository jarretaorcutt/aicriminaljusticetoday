Study: language models reading parole hearing transcripts predicted rearrest about as well as a statistical tool, but their stated reasons didn't match

The lead researcher says the results do not mean such models are ready to make parole decisions.

By the AI in Criminal Justice Today newsroom · Published November 21, 2026
Story type
Research
Jurisdiction
United States, Missouri, California
AI application
large language models, risk assessment
AI framework
Not yet labeled

Large language models can estimate rearrest risk from parole hearing transcripts about as well as a purpose-built statistical model, but their explanations cannot yet be trusted, according to research announced by the University of Missouri-Kansas City on Oct 6, 2026.

Law professor Ryan Copus and UC Davis researcher Hannah Laqueur prompted six LLMs to assess rearrest risk using 141 parole hearing transcripts, the university said, and compared the results with a statistical model trained on more than 4,000 cases with 91 administrative variables. One model's prediction was closer to the actual outcome in 83 of the 141 cases, or 59%. The university says much of the advantage came in cases where the statistical model overestimated risk.

The warning is about reasons. In its written explanations the model emphasized remorse and insight, while the researchers' analysis found its predictions more closely tracked criminal history and the severity of the original offense.

Copus cautioned against using the tools for decisions. "There are too many unknowns for widespread deployment of LLMs to make high-stakes decisions," he said in the release. The paper is titled "Computational Clinical Judgment: Predicting Risk with Large Language Models." We relied on the university's announcement and have not reviewed the paper.

What we know

  • Six language models were tested on 141 parole hearing transcripts (UMKC release, Oct. 6, 2026).
  • One model was closer to the actual outcome than the statistical model in 83 of the 141 cases, or 59% (UMKC release).
  • The model's stated explanations did not match the factors its predictions tracked (UMKC release).

What remains unknown

  • Which jurisdiction's hearings were studied; the release does not say.
  • Whether the result holds on larger samples; counts in the paper's online abstract differ from the release, and we have not reviewed the paper.
  • How the models perform across racial or other groups.

Benefits and opportunities

  • The models did best where a standard tool overestimated risk, which could matter for people held longer than needed.

Risks and limitations

  • A persuasive written explanation that does not reflect the real basis of a score could mislead a parole board.
  • The study measured rearrest, which is not the same as conviction or violence.
