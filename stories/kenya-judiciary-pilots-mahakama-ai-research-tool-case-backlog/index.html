Kenya's Judiciary pilots Mahakama AI, an in-house research tool for judges, as it faces nearly 580,000 pending cases

The courts say they are finalising an AI policy that would bar judicial officers from putting confidential case data into unapproved systems, The Star reports.

Kenya's Judiciary has developed and piloted Mahakama AI, an internal legal research tool meant to help judges and judicial officers sift through precedents and speed up decisions, The Star reported on 19 November 2026.

The tool arrives under heavy caseload pressure. According to the report, the Judiciary had 579,954 pending matters by the end of June 2026, and 341,412 of them, 58.9 per cent, had been unresolved for more than three years. The Star says the Judiciary is operating at 66 per cent of its approved staffing capacity.

The Judiciary's own report, as described by The Star, flags the "unregulated use of AI tools in judicial research and administration" as an urgent emerging issue and lists risks including unverified AI tools generating research errors, fabricating citations, or leaking confidential case details. In response, the Judiciary says it is finalising an Artificial Intelligence Adoption Policy and Practice Directions that would prohibit judicial officers from uploading confidential case data into unapproved external systems, require human verification of AI-generated citations, and require cybersecurity and data-protection audits before any wider rollout.

The Star does not say how many judges use Mahakama AI or which case types it covers. The tool serves the whole court system, including criminal courts, rather than criminal cases alone.

What we know

  • Kenya's Judiciary developed and piloted Mahakama AI, an internal legal research tool for judges and judicial officers (The Star, 19 November 2026).
  • Pending matters stood at 579,954 at the end of June 2026; 341,412 had been unresolved for more than three years (The Star).
  • The Judiciary is finalising an AI adoption policy and practice directions that bar uploading confidential case data to unapproved systems (The Star).

What remains unknown

  • How many judges use the tool, for which case types, and with what accuracy checks during the pilot.
  • When the AI policy and practice directions will be adopted.

Benefits and opportunities

  • An in-house tool may keep case data inside court systems rather than public chatbots.

Risks and limitations

  • Unverified research output could carry fabricated citations into rulings, a risk the Judiciary itself names.
