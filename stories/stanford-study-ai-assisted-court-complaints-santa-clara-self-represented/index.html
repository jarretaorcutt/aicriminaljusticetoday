Stanford researchers find signs of AI drafting in more than a third of the Santa Clara court complaints they examined

Among self-represented filers in Santa Clara, the share went from 19% in 2024 to 72% in 2026. The researchers warn that detectors can estimate how common AI use is, but cannot reliably pinpoint which filings used it.

Signs of AI-assisted drafting in court complaints rose from near zero in 2022 to more than a third of examined Santa Clara complaints in 2026, researchers at the Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI and the Regulation, Evaluation, and Governance Lab (RegLab) wrote in a summary published on November 19, 2026.

The team studied complaints from four state courts. By 2026, they estimate, the prevalence of AI use in Santa Clara and San Francisco counties in California had risen to 38% and 36%, in non-debt-collection cases. Tulsa and Oklahoma counties in Oklahoma were lower, at 17% and 16% for comparable cases.

People representing themselves showed higher adoption. In Santa Clara, the share of self-represented complaints showing signs of AI use went from 19% in 2024 to 72% in 2026, while the share of attorney-filed complaints went from 5% to 34%, the researchers report.

The authors also describe how unreliable detection can be: one AI detector flagged anywhere from 10% to 98% of the same set of 2026 Santa Clara complaints as AI-written, depending on the settings used. To calibrate, they built a benchmark called PhantomDocket from documented hallucination cases in a database maintained by legal researcher Damien Charlotin, and a method called ScoreMix. They caution that AI detection is most reliable for estimating population prevalence, not for pinpointing which specific filings used AI.

The researchers say their work raises real questions about whether AI is helping litigants who cannot afford attorneys or flooding the courts with inadequate lawyering. They note that over 1,500 U.S. cases have been documented to include legal hallucinations, and argue for better disclosure policies across courts.

What we know

  • Estimated AI use in complaints by 2026: 38% in Santa Clara County and 36% in San Francisco County, in non-debt-collection cases (Stanford HAI, November 19, 2026).
  • Tulsa and Oklahoma counties were at 17% and 16% for comparable cases (Stanford HAI).
  • Santa Clara self-represented complaints went from 19% in 2024 to 72% in 2026; attorney-filed complaints went from 5% to 34% (Stanford HAI).
  • One detector flagged anywhere from 10% to 98% of the same complaints depending on settings (Stanford HAI).

What remains unknown

  • Whether the AI-assisted filings were better or worse than others; the study measures prevalence, not quality or outcomes.
  • Rates in criminal cases; the summary describes complaints such as employment, business and debt collection.
  • Whether the pattern holds outside the four courts studied.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Gives courts a calibrated estimate of how common AI-assisted filing is, instead of anecdotes.
  • AI may be helping people who cannot afford a lawyer file at all, a question the authors raise.

Risks and limitations

  • Detectors have nontrivial false positive and false negative rates, so labeling an individual filing as AI-written is risky, the authors say.
  • A wave of AI-generated filings could strain court time and resources, the authors note.
