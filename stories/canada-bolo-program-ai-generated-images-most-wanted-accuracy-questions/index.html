A police-partnered program shows AI-generated moving images of Canada's 25 most wanted and won't say how they were made

Privacy and criminology experts told CBC there is no way to check the likenesses or count false leads.

By the AI in Criminal Justice Today newsroom · Published November 21, 2026

The Bolo Program, a group that has partnered with police forces in some of Canada's biggest cities since 2018 to publicize wanted suspects, has presented AI-generated, moving images of its top 25 fugitives, CBC News reported on Nov. 20. The images were shown life-sized at a news conference in Toronto.

Bolo says it builds the images from the pictures and descriptions police have of each suspect. Asked how they were generated, executive director Max Langlois said that information was confidential, according to CBC. The program points to results: in 2025, 13 out of 25 of its most wanted suspects were arrested, CBC reports.

Experts interviewed by CBC questioned the practice. David Eliot, a University of Ottawa doctoral candidate who studies AI and security, said the public does not know what data produced the avatars and that invented mannerisms or body types could mislead investigators and the public. Tamir Israel of the Canadian Civil Liberties Association asked how many false leads and accusations come from such campaigns and said what happens to people wrongly identified "doesn't appear to be tracked."

CBC said it had not received answers from Bolo to its follow-up questions. Because the images come from a private organization working alongside police, the rules that bind police use of technology may not apply in the same way, the experts told the broadcaster.

What we know

  • Bolo unveiled AI-generated moving images of its top 25 fugitives in Toronto (CBC News, Nov. 20, 2026).
  • Its executive director said how the images were generated is confidential (CBC News).
  • Civil liberties and criminology experts raised accuracy and oversight concerns (CBC News).

What remains unknown

  • Which AI tool made the images and what data it used.
  • How many tips from these campaigns have pointed at the wrong person.
  • Whether partner police forces reviewed the images before release.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Bolo cites arrests of featured suspects as evidence its campaigns work.

Risks and limitations

  • A synthetic likeness can add details that no photo or witness supplied.
  • No one appears to track false identifications.
