Portland's council president proposes pausing new city AI contracts for a year, which could cap a police AI pilot

The measure from Council President Jamie Dunphy goes before the council Dec. 2.

By the AI in Criminal Justice Today newsroom · Published November 24, 2026
Story type
News
Jurisdiction
United States, Oregon, Portland
AI application
AI screening of digital evidence, Municipal AI procurement
AI framework
Not yet labeled

The Portland City Council will hear a proposal to limit the expansion of artificial-intelligence use by city agencies and pause new AI contracts for a year, The Oregonian/OregonLive reported on Nov. 17, 2026. The measure, drafted by Council President Jamie Dunphy, will go before council Dec. 2.

The proposal could affect a police pilot program that uses AI to screen suspects' data files for child sexual-abuse material, the paper reported. Dunphy said the pilot has a set scope, and that expanding it beyond internet sex crimes, for example to retail theft, would not be allowed.

Police Chief Bob Day said in a statement that the bureau is always looking for ways to work more efficiently, particularly given its staffing levels, while balancing innovation with accountability, transparency and the public's trust, according to the paper.

The Oregonian reported that the ordinance comes after backlash against AI and data centers that helped shape the U.S. midterm elections on Nov. 3.

What we know

  • Council President Jamie Dunphy's measure would pause new AI contracts for a year (The Oregonian/OregonLive).
  • It goes before council Dec. 2 (The Oregonian/OregonLive).
  • A police AI pilot that screens data files for child sexual-abuse material could not expand beyond its set scope, Dunphy said (The Oregonian/OregonLive).

What remains unknown

  • How the full council will vote.
  • Which other city AI uses the pause would affect.

Benefits and opportunities

  • The city would set guardrails on AI use before new contracts are signed (The Oregonian/OregonLive).

Risks and limitations

  • Chief Bob Day pointed to staffing challenges as a reason the bureau looks for new tools (The Oregonian/OregonLive).
