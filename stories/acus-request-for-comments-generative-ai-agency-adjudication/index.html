A federal advisory agency asks the public how judges and parties in agency hearings should be allowed to use generative AI

The Administrative Conference of the United States will use the comments to recommend best practices for federal agencies. Comments are due January 19, 2027.

The Administrative Conference of the United States (ACUS), an independent federal agency that recommends improvements to federal administrative procedure, published a request for comments in the Federal Register on 11/19/2026. It asks how adjudicators, other agency staff, parties and their representatives should use generative AI in federal agency adjudications. The notice uses the term "generative super intelligence," which it says was previously referenced as generative artificial intelligence.

The notice says the responses may inform an ACUS project that will recommend best practices for agencies. It says the number of generative AI use cases in the federal government increased about nine-fold between 2023 and 2024.

The notice lists risks, including tools that hallucinate, filings that cite nonexistent legal authorities, and concerns that the tools could be used to fabricate deepfake evidence. It notes that the Executive Office for Immigration Review, which runs the immigration courts, and the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office have published policies on parties' and representatives' use of the technology.

Among its questions, ACUS asks whether parties should have to disclose when and how they used generative AI, or certify that they reviewed its output, and what consequences should follow improper use. Comments must be received by 10 a.m. (ET) on January 19, 2027. The notice is dated November 16, 2026.

What we know

  • ACUS published a request for comments on generative AI use in federal agency adjudications in the Federal Register on 11/19/2026 (Federal Register).
  • Comments are due by 10 a.m. (ET) on January 19, 2027 (Federal Register).
  • The notice says federal generative AI use cases increased about nine-fold between 2023 and 2024 (Federal Register).
  • The Executive Office for Immigration Review and the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office have published policies on parties' use of the technology (Federal Register).

What remains unknown

  • What ACUS will recommend, and whether agencies will adopt it; ACUS recommendations are not binding.
  • How widely immigration judges and other agency adjudicators already use generative AI; the notice gives no count.

Benefits and opportunities

  • The notice says the technology could help adjudicators summarize lengthy submissions, transcribe hearings and translate documents (Federal Register).

Risks and limitations

  • The notice cites tools that hallucinate, citations to nonexistent legal authorities and possible deepfake evidence (Federal Register).
