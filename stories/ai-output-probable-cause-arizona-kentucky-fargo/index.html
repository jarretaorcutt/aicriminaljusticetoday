Two US judges refuse to let AI output stand in for probable cause, as Fargo defends a $10 million false-arrest suit

An Arizona judge released a DUI suspect because an AI tool helped write the arrest report. A Kentucky federal judge let a wrongful-arrest suit over a face match proceed. Fargo says its officers broke no law.

By the AI in Criminal Justice Today newsroom · Published November 19, 2026 · Updated November 20, 2026
Story type
Legal
Jurisdiction
United States, Arizona, Kentucky, North Dakota
AI application
AI report drafting, facial recognition

Not yet labeled

Maricopa County Superior Court Judge Daniel Fenzel ordered a DUI suspect released at his initial hearing because the arrest report ended with a disclaimer that it was generated with assistance from Truleo AI, ABC News reported on Nov. 13. The judge said the court was "unable to determine probable cause at this time" because he could not weigh whether the allegations were written in whole or in part by generative AI. He added that the case could still go forward. The Arizona Department of Public Safety said its policy treats AI only as an aid, requires the trooper to check the draft against body-worn camera footage, and requires a supervisor to review the report.

In Kentucky, U.S. District Judge S. Chad Meredith refused to dismiss Alain Osorio's lawsuit against Florence police Detective Ryan Kilmer and declined to grant him qualified immunity at this stage, in an order filed on Nov. 9, Tech Times reported. Osorio spent more than two weeks in jail after, he alleges, the detective relied only on a facial recognition result; prosecutors dismissed the charges on May 7, 2025, after he showed he had been working in Nebraska. The judge wrote that constitutional protections do not allow arrests "based on nothing more than an uncorroborated conclusion by AI," according to Tech Times and ID Tech, which cited Reuters. Kilmer denies wrongdoing.

In North Dakota, the city of Fargo and one of its detectives answered a $10 million suit by Angela Lipps, who was held for six months on a bank theft charge after a facial recognition match passed on by West Fargo police, Biometric Update reported on Nov. 17. The city denies violating her rights, says the searches were run by other agencies, and acknowledges that its police had no formal written policy on facial recognition.

What we know

  • Judge Fenzel said he could not determine probable cause from a report drafted with Truleo AI and released the suspect; the case can still proceed (ABC News).
  • Arizona DPS policy requires troopers to verify AI-assisted drafts and supervisors to review them (ABC News).
  • Judge Meredith denied the detective's motion to dismiss and qualified immunity at this stage in Osorio v. Kilmer, No. 2:26-cv-00004, E.D. Ky. (Tech Times; ID Tech).
  • Fargo's filing acknowledges no formal written facial recognition policy and denies constitutional wrongdoing (Biometric Update).

What remains unknown

  • Whether other Arizona courts will treat AI-drafted narratives the same way.
  • How the Osorio and Lipps suits will end. Neither ruling decides the merits.
  • How many agencies use AI report-writing tools without a disclosure line.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Disclosure lines on AI-assisted reports let courts and defense lawyers see when AI was involved.
  • Courts are setting a floor: AI output is a lead, not proof.

Risks and limitations

  • People can be jailed for weeks or months on an unverified match before anyone checks it.
  • Agencies that share matches across departments may each say someone else is responsible.
  • AI-drafted reports without disclosure would be harder to challenge.
