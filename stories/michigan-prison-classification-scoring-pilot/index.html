Michigan will pilot a new scoring system for prison security levels on Dec. 1, and its developer says AI plays no part in the score

The Michigan Department of Corrections says the redesigned classification system, built with the nonprofit Recidiviz, should predict future violence about 30% better. It has not said how it reached that figure.

The Michigan Department of Corrections plans to begin testing a new way of assigning security levels on Dec. 1 at selected men's prisons, Fox News reported on Nov. 10. Security levels shape where a person lives, how closely they are watched and, according to the report, their access to education and job training. The department oversees approximately 32,000 incarcerated people.

The software comes from Recidiviz, a nonprofit that Fox News says supports criminal justice agencies in 19 states. Its team analyzed 10 years of Michigan classification data. The system calculates a score from prison records using factors chosen by Michigan corrections experts, gives more weight to recent disciplinary incidents than old ones, and can flag a record for review when new information arrives. Staff can review the calculation and override a score, Fox News reported.

In its Oct. 28 announcement, the department said the system should improve its ability to predict future violence by approximately 30%. Neither Fox News nor Futurism found an explanation of how that figure was calculated. Futurism reported on Nov. 1 that the department's press release had since been deleted.

Early coverage described the tool as AI. Recidiviz published an explanation on Nov. 2 titled "Why We Don't Use AI to Classify People in Prisons," according to Fox News, and a department spokesperson, Jenni Riehle, told Futurism that AI is never used to decide a person's security level or release. Both outlets note that Recidiviz does use AI elsewhere, for example for meeting transcription.

Byron Osborn, president of the union representing Michigan corrections officers, told reporters the bigger problem is a shortage of higher-security beds, Fox News reported. The state plans to add 870 high-security beds.

What we know

  • Testing is planned to begin Dec. 1 at selected men's prisons (Fox News).
  • Recidiviz built the system after analyzing 10 years of Michigan classification data (Fox News).
  • The department expects about a 30% improvement in predicting future violence; the method behind that figure is not published (Fox News; Futurism).
  • Recidiviz and the department say the classification score is rules-based and AI is not used to decide security level or release (Fox News; Futurism).
  • Staff can review and override scores (Fox News).

What remains unknown

  • Which prisons are in the pilot, and for how long.
  • How the 30% figure was calculated, and what error rates the formula has.
  • How errors in disciplinary records will be caught before they affect a score.
  • Whether results will be published before any expansion.

Benefits and opportunities

  • More frequent reviews could move people to lower security sooner when their behavior changes, according to the coverage.
  • A points-based formula that staff can inspect is more transparent than a model whose reasoning cannot be seen.

Risks and limitations

  • A higher score can limit access to education and job training, so scoring errors carry real costs.
  • Wrong information in prison records would flow straight into scores.
  • Better scores do not help if there are no beds at the right security level, the officers' union says.
