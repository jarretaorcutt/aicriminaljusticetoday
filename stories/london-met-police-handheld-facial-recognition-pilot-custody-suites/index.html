London's Met Police starts a six-month pilot of handheld facial recognition on the street and in custody suites

One hundred officers can check a photo against the force's custody images; the UK biometrics commissioner says national oversight plans still lack detail.

London's Metropolitan Police has begun a six-month pilot that lets officers photograph a person and run the image against the force's custody image database to confirm identity. The force announced the trial of what it calls Operator Initiated Facial Recognition in a release published Nov. 20, saying it is being trialled by 100 trained officers across custody suites and frontline policing.

The Met says the system returns a result only where it finds a match above a threshold informed by independent testing, and that "Every potential match is reviewed by a trained officer" alongside other information. Commander Nick John is the force's lead for the project. The release says the pilot will be monitored for any evidence of disproportionality, and it cites more than 3,000 arrests from the force's separate live facial recognition deployments since 2024.

Biometric Update reports that the pilot began November 17, that the algorithm is NEC's NeoFace, and that a 32-page policy document sets the rules, including deletion of probe images within 31 days after a completed search. The outlet says the Met will treat the pilot as a proof of concept and intends afterward to add searches against an unsolved-crime database.

The pilot starts while the legal framework is unsettled. In a separate report on Nov. 18, Biometric Update said the UK Biometrics and Surveillance Camera Commissioner, William Webster, welcomed the government's move toward primary legislation on police facial recognition but said detail is missing on necessity and proportionality, on who judges efficacy and on whether a new oversight body will be resourced. He wants those questions answered before the Police Reform Bill is laid before Parliament. The outlet cites a count of at least 13 police forces in England and Wales that have or use live facial recognition.

The Met's release quotes no critics. We did not review the 32-page policy.

What we know

  • 100 trained officers are using handheld facial recognition in a six-month pilot covering custody suites and frontline policing (Metropolitan Police release, Nov. 20, 2026).
  • The pilot began November 17 and uses NEC's NeoFace; probe images are deleted within 31 days after a completed search (Biometric Update).
  • The UK biometrics commissioner says government oversight plans for police facial recognition need more detail (Biometric Update, Nov. 18, 2026).

What remains unknown

  • The match threshold and the error rates measured in testing.
  • What happens when a person refuses to be photographed; we did not review the policy document.
  • Whether the pilot's results will be published.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Faster identification in custody could surface risks such as self-harm markers sooner, as the force argues.
  • A written policy, a fixed deletion period and human review are stated safeguards.

Risks and limitations

  • The pilot runs before Parliament has set a legal framework for police facial recognition.
  • The Met plans to widen the databases searched after the pilot.
