Lowell's Flock plate-reader data was searched more than 1.5 million times in three months, audit logs show

WBZ NewsRadio obtained audit logs for April 1 to July 1, 2026; about 3,000 agencies appear in the network audit.

By the AI in Criminal Justice Today newsroom · Published November 24, 2026
Story type
News
Jurisdiction
United States, Massachusetts, Lowell
AI application
Automated license plate recognition

WBZ NewsRadio reports that Lowell's Flock license plate camera network was searched by local, state and federal police agencies nationwide more than 1.5 million times in the three months from April 1 to July 1, 2026, based on audit logs it obtained from the Lowell Police Department after multiple requests. The station reports that the network audit shows data sharing with about 3,000 agencies across the country, with Lowell's data sifted through at least 500,000 times a month, and that two federal agencies appear in the logs: the US Postal Inspection Service, which made thousands of queries, and Midwest HIDTA, a drug trafficking taskforce.

The station reports that the Lowell City Council shut the network down on Sept. 23 after a 6-5 vote, then voted 8-1 on Oct. 6, in executive session, to reactivate it with the caveat that camera data not be automatically shared with other agencies; Councilor Sean McDonough cast the only vote against. Deputy Superintendent Mark LeBlanc told WBZ that automatic network sharing was disabled on 9/23/2026, and that police policy still allows ALPR data to be released to other authorized and verified law enforcement agencies on request. WBZ notes that an entry from an agency does not necessarily mean it was looking for people in Lowell, and that Lowell Police declined to include the reason for each search in the network files, citing the volume.

What we know

  • Lowell's Flock network was searched by outside and local agencies more than 1.5 million times between April 1 and July 1, 2026 (WBZ NewsRadio).
  • The network audit shows data sharing with about 3,000 agencies, including the US Postal Inspection Service and Midwest HIDTA (WBZ NewsRadio).
  • Automatic network sharing was disabled on 9/23/2026, but data can still be released to other agencies on request, per Deputy Superintendent Mark LeBlanc (WBZ NewsRadio).

What remains unknown

  • Why each outside search was run; Lowell Police did not include search reasons in the network files.
  • How many of the searches were looking for people or vehicles in Lowell.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Police leadership told WBZ the network has been helpful in solving crime (WBZ NewsRadio).

Risks and limitations

  • Local plate-reader data can be searched by thousands of outside agencies, including federal ones, with little local visibility (WBZ NewsRadio).
