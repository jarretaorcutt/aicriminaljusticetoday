A federal evidence rule for AI-generated material is back in revision and unlikely to take effect before late 2028, an ABA update says

Until then, judges are testing AI evidence under the existing expert-reliability rule.

By the AI in Criminal Justice Today newsroom · Published November 21, 2026

The proposed federal rule for machine-generated evidence is still being reworked, according to an update by Amy Sellars published by the American Bar Association's Litigation Section on Nov 18, 2026. She writes that the Standing Committee sent Rule 707 back for revision in June 2026, that the revised draft adds a notice requirement and went to a mini-conference on October 15, and that it is unlikely any version can take effect before late 2028.

Rule 707 would put machine-generated evidence through the reliability test that Rule 702 applies to expert witnesses. In the meantime, Sellars writes, courts are applying existing rules. She cites State v. Puloka, in which a Washington court excluded AI-enhanced video, and Matter of Weber, in which a New York court rejected an expert's calculations made with an AI assistant in part because he could not explain how it worked.

The piece is a practitioner's update and includes her view that Rule 702 may be enough. For criminal cases, the practical point is that prosecutors or defendants offering AI-processed evidence should expect to produce validation studies, error rates and the version of the tool used.

What we know

  • Proposed Rule 707 was sent back for revision in June 2026 and the revised draft adds a notice requirement (ABA Litigation Section, Nov. 18, 2026).
  • The author says no version is likely to take effect before late 2028 (ABA Litigation Section).
  • Courts have excluded AI-enhanced or AI-assisted evidence under existing rules in the cases she cites (ABA Litigation Section).

What remains unknown

  • The text of the revised draft; we did not review the committee's materials.
  • Whether the committee will advance the rule at all.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Existing reliability rules already give judges a way to test AI evidence.

Risks and limitations

  • Without a dedicated rule, standards may differ from court to court for several more years.
