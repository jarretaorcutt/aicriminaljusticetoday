UMKC and UC Davis researchers find large language models predicted rearrest as well as, and sometimes better than, statistical risk tools

Law professor Ryan Copus says the models' explanations of their predictions were not faithful, KC Roo News reports.

By the AI in Criminal Justice Today newsroom · Published November 24, 2026

UMKC law professor Ryan Copus and UC Davis researcher Hannah Laqueur have studied for more than a year and a half whether AI can predict rearrests, KC Roo News reported on Nov. 23, 2026. Their research compares traditional risk-assessment tools, which use data such as age and criminal history, with large language models.

Copus said the models "at least matched, and in some cases beat, pretty sophisticated statistical models," and that he and Laqueur were surprised. He said many parole systems nationwide already use statistical tools that give a percentage chance of rearrest, but that those tools cannot explain their reasoning and vendors often call their methods trade secrets. Large language models are also hard to interpret: Copus called them "black boxes." When the researchers asked the models to explain their predictions and analyzed the explanations, they were not faithful, he said.

Copus argued that comparisons should weigh human bias alongside algorithmic bias. He said next steps are not settled, though the researchers want to dig into why the explanations are unfaithful. The report does not say where the research is published or which models were tested.

What we know

  • Copus and Laqueur compared traditional risk tools with large language models for predicting rearrest (KC Roo News).
  • Copus says the models at least matched, and in some cases beat, sophisticated statistical models (KC Roo News).
  • The researchers analyzed the models' explanations and found they were not faithful, per Copus (KC Roo News).

What remains unknown

  • Which models, datasets and accuracy figures the study used; the article gives none.
  • Whether the work has been peer reviewed or published.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Researchers say comparing models to human judgment is a fairer test than judging machines alone.

Risks and limitations

  • Models whose reasoning cannot be examined are hard to challenge if used in parole or sentencing decisions.
  • Explanations that do not reflect how a prediction was made could mislead people who rely on them.
