Swiss forensic psychiatrists have used risk-scoring tools in more than 100 Federal Supreme Court rulings since 2000, a Swissinfo analysis finds

Many of the tools are statistical scores rather than AI, Swissinfo reports, and experts say few have been validated on Swiss data.

By the AI in Criminal Justice Today newsroom · Published November 24, 2026

Jurisdiction

Swissinfo's analysis found that forensic psychiatrists have used one or more algorithmic instruments to assess dangerousness and risk of reoffending in more than a hundred Federal Supreme Court rulings since 2000, according to the outlet. The tools are often not based on artificial intelligence, Swissinfo notes; they calculate a statistical risk from factors such as age, prior convictions and psychological traits.

Swissinfo cites a study of 307 men subject to criminal measures at the Pöschwies prison in the canton of Zurich, which found that use of algorithmic tools in forensic psychiatric assessments rose from 20% in the early 2000s to 100% since 2017. Thierry Urwyler, a criminal law professor at the University of Zurich and an author of that study, estimates that almost all forensic psychiatric assessments in German-speaking Switzerland now use at least one such tool. He says robust data are lacking: some of the most common tools have been validated by their developers but not by independent studies, and others rely on recidivism statistics gathered abroad. One example Swissinfo gives is the Violence Risk Appraisal Guide (VRAG), developed in Canada, where a study found recidivism rates are higher than in Switzerland. Urwyler says using it without Swiss data could push judges toward stricter measures.

Swissinfo also describes a Lucerne case in which a high score on a psychopathy-measuring tool supported an assessment of severe personality disorder. A second assessment years later disagreed, according to former Lucerne cantonal court judge Marianne Heer, who says the man spent almost ten years in security detention without justification. The court ordered his release and the Federal Supreme Court upheld that decision. Swissinfo adds that the Federal Supreme Court has in some cases questioned how much weight such tools carried, but that the criticism appears to have changed forensic practice little.

What we know

  • Swissinfo's analysis found use of one or more algorithmic instruments in more than a hundred Federal Supreme Court rulings since 2000 (Swissinfo).
  • A study of 307 men at Pöschwies prison found use rose from 20% in the early 2000s to 100% since 2017, per Urwyler (Swissinfo).
  • Many of the tools compute statistical risk and are often not based on artificial intelligence (Swissinfo).
  • The Federal Supreme Court has in some cases questioned the tools' influence on assessments (Swissinfo).

What remains unknown

  • How often the tools change the outcome of a decision on detention or release; Swissinfo gives one case, not a rate.
  • Which specific tools are used in which cantons beyond the examples named.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Structured tools can add consistency to risk assessments, which is the argument their users make.

Risks and limitations

  • Tools validated by developers or on foreign data may overstate risk for people in Switzerland, according to Urwyler.
  • Scores that look precise are hard for the defense to challenge, a defense lawyer told Swissinfo.
