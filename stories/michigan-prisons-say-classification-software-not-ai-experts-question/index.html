Michigan's prison agency and its contractor say the new prisoner classification software is not AI; two of three experts question that

The system, to be piloted starting Dec. 1, uses a points formula built from 10 years of state data. One professor told the Detroit Free Press it is "definitely an automated decision system" and a form of AI.

Jurisdiction

Michigan's new system for setting prisoners' security levels does not use artificial intelligence, according to the Michigan Department of Corrections (MDOC) and its contractor, Utah-based Recidiviz. Experts interviewed by the Detroit Free Press are less sure, the paper reported on Nov. 18, 2026.

According to the Free Press, MDOC Director Heidi Washington announced on Oct. 28 that the agency was using new software to "improve MDOC's ability to more accurately predict incarcerated individuals' risk of future violence by approximately 30%." The system is being piloted starting Dec. 1.

Recidiviz said in a Nov. 2 blog post that its classification module uses fixed rules, approved by the MDOC, to calculate a prisoner's security classification from Level 1, the lowest, to Level 5, the highest, the Free Press reports. To set the new scoring, the company analyzed 10 years of the state's classification data to see which factors matter and how much weight each should get. A Recidiviz spokesperson told the paper there was no use of machine learning, and that analysts relied on "correlational and regression statistics." The paper reports that the MDOC is accessing the Recidiviz platform through a $4-million state contract, and that Recidiviz acknowledges using AI in other modules.

The new system is largely automated: it updates calculations as new information comes in and alerts staff when a classification needs review, with humans making the ultimate decisions, according to statements by the MDOC and Recidiviz cited by the Free Press. The spokesperson said the data points include a prisoner's age but not race or gender.

Siwei Lyu, who directs the Institute for Artificial Intelligence and Data Science at the University at Buffalo, told the paper that Recidiviz "appears to use a traditional points-based algorithm rather than an AI model" to calculate individual scores, but that developing the scoring rules through statistical analysis of historical data could involve machine learning, and he would need more details to say. Sylvester Johnson, a Northwestern University professor, said the system is "definitely an automated decision system" and a form of AI. Hayden Smith, a University of South Carolina professor, said it appeared a form of AI was used to interpret the ten years of data, though he would need more information to say anything definitive.

The Free Press notes that classification affects whether people are housed alone or with others, their freedom of movement, their visits and their eligibility for prison programs.

What we know

  • The MDOC and Recidiviz say the classification system does not use AI (Detroit Free Press, Nov. 18, 2026).
  • The system is being piloted starting Dec. 1 and scores security classification from Level 1 to Level 5 using fixed rules approved by the MDOC (Free Press, citing a Nov. 2 Recidiviz blog post).
  • Recidiviz analyzed 10 years of state classification data to set the scoring, and says it used correlational and regression statistics, not machine learning (Free Press).
  • Of three experts interviewed, two questioned and one rejected the claim that the system is not AI (Free Press).

What remains unknown

  • The detailed methodology behind the new scoring; two of the experts said they would need more information.
  • How the claimed improvement of approximately 30% in predicting violence was measured.
  • How people in prison will be able to see and challenge their scores once the pilot begins.

Benefits and opportunities

  • Recidiviz says the single scoring system should be simpler to understand, with the point value of each factor clear to staff and prisoners (Free Press).

Risks and limitations

  • One expert told the Free Press that proprietary software replacing standardized forms could make it harder for prisoners to challenge their scores.
  • Whether a tool is labeled AI can decide which safeguards and disclosure rules apply to it.
